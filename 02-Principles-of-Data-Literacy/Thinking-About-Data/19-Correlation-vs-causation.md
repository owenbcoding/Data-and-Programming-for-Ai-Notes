# Correlation vs. Causation

## The Fundamental Distinction
Correlation indicates that two variables are related, while causation means that one variable directly causes changes in the other. This is one of the most important concepts in data analysis.

## Understanding Correlation

### What Correlation Shows
- Variables move together
- Statistical association
- Predictive relationship
- Pattern in data
- Mathematical relationship

### What Correlation Does NOT Show
- Which variable causes the other
- Direction of influence
- Mechanism of action
- Underlying cause
- Causal relationship

### Types of Correlation
- **Spurious**: Apparent relationship by chance
- **Indirect**: Through third variable
- **Coincidental**: Random association
- **Meaningful**: Genuine association
- **Causal**: One causes the other

## Understanding Causation

### What Causation Requires
- Temporal precedence (cause precedes effect)
- Mechanism (plausible explanation)
- Strength of association
- Dose-response relationship
- Consistency across studies

### Establishing Causation
- **Experimental design**: Gold standard
- **Randomized controlled trials**: Strong evidence
- **Longitudinal studies**: Time sequence
- **Natural experiments**: Real-world settings
- **Statistical controls**: Accounting for confounders

### Bradford Hill Criteria
- Strength of association
- Consistency
- Specificity
- Temporality
- Biological gradient
- Plausibility
- Coherence
- Experiment
- Analogy

## Common Fallacies

### Post Hoc Fallacy
- "After this, therefore because of this"
- Assuming temporal sequence implies causation
- Common in everyday reasoning
- Ignores alternative explanations
- Logical error

### Cum Hoc Fallacy
- "With this, therefore because of this"
- Assuming correlation implies causation
- Very common mistake
- Ignores confounding variables
- Poor statistical reasoning

### Third Variable Problem
- Hidden variable causes both
- Spurious correlation
- Confounding variable
- Mediating variable
- Need for controls

## Examples of Spurious Correlations

### Classic Examples
- Ice cream sales and drowning deaths
- Both increase in summer
- Temperature is the real cause
- No causal relationship
- Common confounding

### Humorous Examples
- Number of storks and birth rates
- Miss America age and steam deaths
- Cheese consumption and tangled bedsheets
- Nicholas Cage movies and swimming pool deaths
- Demonstrates the problem

### Serious Examples
- Medical treatments and recovery
- Economic policies and outcomes
- Educational interventions and performance
- Public health measures and disease rates
- Important policy implications

## Confounding Variables

### What are Confounders?
- Variables that affect both variables of interest
- Create apparent causal relationship
- Can be measured or unmeasured
- Can invalidate causal claims
- Must be controlled

### Types of Confounders
- **Measured**: Can be controlled statistically
- **Unmeasured**: Cannot be controlled
- **Known**: Recognized potential confounders
- **Unknown**: Hidden confounders
- **Complex**: Multiple interacting confounders

### Controlling for Confounders
- **Randomization**: Best method
- **Stratification**: Separate analysis
- **Statistical adjustment**: Regression
- **Matching**: Create comparable groups
- **Propensity scores**: Advanced method

## Establishing Causal Relationships

### Study Design Hierarchy
1. **Randomized experiments**: Strongest evidence
2. **Quasi-experiments**: Moderate evidence
3. **Longitudinal studies**: Good evidence
4. **Cross-sectional studies**: Weak evidence
5. **Case studies**: Weakest evidence

### Statistical Approaches
- **Instrumental variables**: Natural experiments
- **Regression discontinuity**: Threshold effects
- **Difference-in-differences**: Before/after comparison
- **Structural equation modeling**: Complex relationships
- **Causal inference methods**: Modern approaches

### Practical Criteria
- **Temporal sequence**: Cause precedes effect
- **Strength**: Strong association
- **Consistency**: Replicable findings
- **Specificity**: Specific cause-effect
- **Biological plausibility**: Makes sense

## Reverse Causation

### What is Reverse Causation?
- Effect influences supposed cause
- Direction of causality reversed
- Common in observational data
- Hard to detect
- Important consideration

### Examples
- Health and wealth
- Education and income
- Mental health and substance use
- Crime and poverty
- Complex bidirectional relationships

### Detection
- Longitudinal data helps
- Theoretical understanding
- Temporal sequence analysis
- Instrumental variables
- Causal modeling

## Best Practices

### Analysis
- Always consider alternative explanations
- Look for confounding variables
- Examine temporal sequence
- Use appropriate study designs
- Be cautious about causal claims

### Interpretation
- Distinguish correlation from causation
- Acknowledge limitations
- Consider third variables
- Examine biological plausibility
- Be transparent about uncertainty

### Communication
- Clearly state relationship type
- Avoid causal language for correlation
- Explain alternative explanations
- Discuss limitations
- Use precise terminology

### Research Design
- Use experimental methods when possible
- Randomize to control confounders
- Collect longitudinal data
- Measure potential confounders
- Pre-register analysis plans
